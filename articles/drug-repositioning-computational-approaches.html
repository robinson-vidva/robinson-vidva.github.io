Drug Repositioning Through Computational Modeling

How computational modeling finds new uses for existing drugs

Drug Discovery Computational Biology February 1, 2026 8 min read — views

For most of my first twelve years in computational biology I worked on one question, asked in different diseases: which drugs we already have could work on a condition they were not made for? This piece describes how we approached it at Cellworks and later at Digirobi Solutions, what the models got right, and where they stopped being useful.

Repositioning is attractive for a plain reason. An approved drug comes with years of safety, dosing, and manufacturing data, so most of the expensive unknowns are already settled. What is not settled is whether it will work somewhere new. That is a prediction problem, and it is where modeling helps.

Note: This article describes computational research methods and published, peer-reviewed work. It is for scientific and educational purposes only and is not medical advice. The drugs, compounds, and combinations discussed are research findings, many validated only in computational models, cell lines, or animal studies, and should not be used to guide treatment or self-medication. Always consult a qualified healthcare professional.

Building a disease you can run

The models were networks of ordinary differential equations assembled from published experiments. A cell type was a set of signaling, metabolic, and transcriptional pathways, and a disease was that network with the relevant genes pushed up or down. For rheumatoid arthritis the unit was the inflamed joint, so the model held eight cell types exchanging cytokines. For cancer the unit was a single tumor cell with its mutations written in.

Drugs entered the model the way they act on cells: as inhibition or activation of their known targets, with downstream effects taken from the literature. Over several years we built a library of about 200 drug models, each with its targets, mechanism, pharmacokinetics, and known resistance routes. A drug written once could then be applied to any disease network. That reuse is what made repositioning practical.

Every model had to earn its use. We checked it against perturbation experiments from the literature before trusting it with a new question, and that habit mattered more than any single prediction.

Inflammation first

My first work in this area, in 2008, was a model of how a soluble epoxide hydrolase inhibitor dampens inflammatory signaling [1]. The rheumatoid arthritis program grew out of it. We used the joint model to look for pairs of approved drugs whose effects converged on the pathways driving the disease. Two combinations looked synergistic in the model, and when tested in a mouse collagen-induced arthritis model, one of them reduced disease progression [2], [3]. The compositions were filed as patent applications [4], [5].

The same modeling fed into collaborations on oral mucosal inflammation, where the question was how two antimicrobial peptides, beta-defensin 3 and histatin 5, change the chemokine response of immune cells to a bacterial protein [6], [7]. Those were not repositioning studies, but they were good tests of whether a multi-cell model could reproduce what a laboratory measured.

Cancer: from a combination to a patient

In oncology the same machinery was pointed at tumors. One early result was a combination of three drugs from outside oncology, nelfinavir (an HIV protease inhibitor), metformin, and rosuvastatin, for cancers with PTEN or TP53 aberrations. In the model the three acted together on survival pathways those tumors depend on. I drafted the claims for that patent and took it through the examiner's objections; it was granted in 2018 [8].

To test predictions against patients rather than cell lines, we worked with the Beat AML project at Oregon Health & Science University. Each patient's mutations and copy-number changes were written into the tumor-cell network, the drug was applied in the model, and the predicted response was compared with how that patient's cells responded to the same drug in the laboratory. For the BET inhibitor JQ1, the prediction agreed with the laboratory result in 93 of 100 patients [9]. The same comparison was run for venetoclax, CDK4/6 inhibitors, IDH inhibitors, and dasatinib [10], [11], [12], [13]. The JQ1 study is walked through in detail in Digital twins in medicine, with a leukemia example.

Two things from that work have stayed with me. A model that only reports an agreement rate is hard to trust; what convinced clinicians was opening the network and seeing why a patient was predicted to respond. And the misses were informative. Many came from mutations with no known functional effect, which the model had no way to represent.

The multi-cell work continued alongside. Models of epithelial, dendritic, and T cells reproduced measured chemokine and cytokine responses for 15 of 20 readouts after one inflammatory stimulus and 16 of 20 after another [14].

Plant compounds alongside chemotherapy

At Digirobi Solutions, the consultancy I ran from 2019 to 2022, the approach was applied to a different pool of compounds. For a nutrigenomics platform we assembled databases of cancer genomics, drugs, and plant-derived compounds, and modeled which of those compounds act on pathways complementary to standard treatments for blood cancers. The resulting combinations are covered by a granted US patent and its European counterpart [15], [16]. As the note above says, these are patent claims and model results, not treatment advice.

What I would tell someone starting now

  • Validate before you predict. A model that has not reproduced known experiments has no business suggesting new ones.
  • Keep the mechanism readable. Partners acted on predictions they could trace through the network, not on scores.
  • Expect the data to be the limit. Many of the wrong calls I saw came from incomplete inputs, not wrong equations.
  • Remember what a preclinical result is. A combination that slows arthritis in mice, or matches a cell assay, is a reason to run a trial. It is not a treatment.
  • Think about patents early. A new use for an old drug can be protected, but only if the claims are written with the evidence in hand.

The tools are different today. Public resources such as Open Targets and openFDA make a first pass at repositioning possible in a browser, which is why I built drugtargets. The hard part has not changed: deciding which predictions deserve an experiment.
