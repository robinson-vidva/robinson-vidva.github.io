Drug Repositioning Through Computational Modeling

A Practitioner's Perspective on Accelerating Drug Discovery

Drug Discovery Computational Biology February 1, 2026 8 min read

Developing a new drug from scratch typically takes over a decade and costs billions of dollars, with a high rate of failure. Drug repositioning — the strategy of finding new therapeutic uses for existing, approved medications — offers a compelling alternative that can dramatically reduce both timelines and costs. Having spent over 18 years working at the intersection of computational biology and drug discovery, I have seen firsthand how computational modeling is transforming this field.

In this article, I share my perspective on computational approaches to drug repositioning, drawing from my experience with predictive modeling platforms, multi-drug combination design, and precision medicine workflows across oncology and inflammatory diseases.

Why Drug Repositioning Matters

Traditional drug development follows a long, costly path: target identification, lead compound screening, preclinical testing, and multi-phase clinical trials. The attrition rate is staggering — roughly 90% of candidates that enter clinical trials never reach patients.

Drug repositioning sidesteps much of this risk. Because repositioned drugs already have established safety profiles, pharmacokinetic data, and manufacturing processes, they can move to clinical evaluation far more quickly. The key challenge shifts from safety validation to efficacy prediction: which existing drugs might work for a different disease, and why?

This is precisely where computational methods become indispensable. Rather than relying solely on serendipitous clinical observations (as was historically the case), we can now systematically screen thousands of approved compounds against disease-specific molecular signatures to identify promising candidates.

Computational Biology Modeling: Building a Virtual Disease

One of the most powerful approaches I have worked with involves constructing detailed computational models of disease biology. Rather than viewing a disease through a single pathway or target, these models integrate multiple layers of biological data — genomic, proteomic, and signaling pathway information — to create a comprehensive representation of the disease state.

The principle is straightforward: if you can accurately simulate the molecular machinery driving a disease, you can computationally test how different drugs (alone or in combination) might disrupt that machinery. The model predicts drug responses by simulating how a compound interacts with proteins, alters signaling cascades, and ultimately affects cellular behavior.

In my work on acute myeloid leukemia (AML) through the Beat AML project, this approach proved its value. We used computational biology models to predict patient responses to various targeted therapies — including BET inhibitors, CDK4/6 inhibitors, dasatinib, venetoclax, and IDH inhibitors — and validated these predictions against actual patient-derived drug sensitivity data. The ability to predict which patients would respond to which therapies, based on their individual molecular profiles, represents a meaningful step toward precision oncology.

The Power of Combination Therapy Design

Cancer and complex diseases rarely respond to single-agent therapies in a durable way. Tumor cells adapt, compensatory pathways activate, and resistance emerges. This biological reality makes rational combination therapy design one of the most impactful applications of computational drug repositioning.

Through computational modeling, we can simulate how multiple drugs interact with overlapping or complementary pathways. This allows us to identify synergistic combinations — where the combined effect is greater than the sum of individual drug effects — and to predict which combinations are most likely to overcome resistance mechanisms.

My work on combinations of repositioned drugs for cancer treatment illustrates this approach. By modeling the molecular effects of nelfinavir (an HIV protease inhibitor), metformin (a diabetes medication), and rosuvastatin (a cholesterol-lowering statin) on cancer cells with specific genetic aberrations in PTEN and TP53 tumor suppressor pathways, we demonstrated that this combination of three already-approved drugs could target cancer through mechanisms entirely different from their original therapeutic purpose. This work resulted in a granted US patent (US10098880B2).

The key insight is that each of these drugs, designed for completely different conditions, affects molecular pathways that converge on cancer cell survival. Computational modeling allowed us to identify this convergence and predict synergy — something that would be extraordinarily difficult through traditional screening alone.

From Oncology to Inflammatory Disease

The same computational principles apply beyond cancer. Inflammatory diseases, driven by dysregulated immune signaling, present an equally rich landscape for drug repositioning.

In our work on rheumatoid arthritis, we used predictive software-based mathematical modeling to design novel combination therapies from existing repurposed drugs. These computationally designed combinations were then validated in murine collagen-induced arthritis models, where they showed significant impact on disease progression. This work demonstrated that computational modeling could guide the repositioning of existing therapies for inflammatory conditions, not just oncology.

Similarly, our research on oral inflammatory responses and immune signaling involved building computational models that could accurately predict how the immune system responds to bacterial challenges and how different compounds modulate those responses. These models provided insights into how existing drugs might be repurposed to manage chronic inflammatory conditions.

Integrating Natural Compounds

An emerging dimension of computational drug repositioning involves the systematic evaluation of natural compounds alongside conventional drugs. Many plant-derived molecules have well-characterized mechanisms of action but have not been rigorously evaluated in combination with standard therapeutics.

Our work on scientifically matched plant supplements combined with antineoplastic compounds for hematological malignancies (US patent US11478444B2) exemplifies this approach. Rather than treating natural products as alternative medicine, we used computational modeling to identify specific plant-derived compounds whose mechanisms of action could complement or enhance standard cancer therapies. The computational framework ensured these combinations were scientifically rational, with predicted synergistic effects based on molecular pathway analysis.

This approach opens a vast, underexplored space for drug repositioning: the systematic, evidence-based combination of approved drugs with characterized natural compounds.

Precision Medicine and Patient-Specific Predictions

Perhaps the most exciting application of computational drug repositioning is in precision medicine — predicting which repositioned drugs will work for specific patients based on their individual molecular profiles.

In the Beat AML project, we worked with patient-specific genomic data to generate individualized drug response predictions. The workflow involved: (1) characterizing each patient's molecular profile, (2) simulating how their specific tumor biology would respond to different drugs and combinations, and (3) comparing these predictions with actual ex vivo drug sensitivity testing results.

This patient-by-patient approach is fundamentally different from traditional repositioning, which asks "what disease could this drug treat?" Instead, it asks "which of these available drugs would work best for this specific patient?" It transforms drug repositioning from a population-level strategy into a personalized therapeutic tool.

Challenges and Practical Considerations

While computational drug repositioning holds enormous promise, several challenges remain:

  • Model validation is essential. Computational predictions are only as reliable as the models generating them. Every prediction must be validated experimentally — first in cell lines or patient-derived samples, then in animal models, and ultimately in clinical settings. My experience has reinforced that the most impactful computational work is tightly integrated with experimental validation.
  • Data quality and availability. The accuracy of predictive models depends on the quality and breadth of the underlying biological data. Genomic, proteomic, and clinical datasets are improving rapidly, but gaps remain, particularly for rare diseases and underrepresented populations.
  • Regulatory and intellectual property pathways. Repositioning existing drugs introduces unique regulatory considerations. New combinations of approved drugs may require novel clinical trial designs, and intellectual property protection for repositioned uses can be complex. My experience navigating the patent landscape for drug combinations has highlighted the importance of early IP strategy in repositioning programs.
  • Translating from model to clinic. A computationally predicted drug response must ultimately work in a living patient. Pharmacokinetics, drug-drug interactions, tissue penetration, and patient compliance all introduce variables that computational models may not fully capture. Close collaboration between computational scientists and clinicians is critical.

Looking Ahead

The convergence of several trends is accelerating the impact of computational drug repositioning. Large-scale patient genomic databases, improvements in machine learning for drug-target interaction prediction, multi-omics data integration, and increasing availability of real-world clinical data are all expanding what computational models can achieve.

In my current work, I continue to apply computational approaches to understand disease biology and identify therapeutic opportunities. The tools and methods have evolved significantly since I began this work over 18 years ago, but the core principle remains: by building rigorous computational models of disease, we can systematically discover new uses for existing drugs and bring effective therapies to patients faster.

Drug repositioning will not replace traditional drug development, but it represents an increasingly important complement — one where computational biology plays a central, driving role.
