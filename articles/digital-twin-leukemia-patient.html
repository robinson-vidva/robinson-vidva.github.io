Digital twins in medicine, with a leukemia example

What a digital twin is, how one was built for 100 AML patients, and what it showed about a BET inhibitor

Research Precision Medicine April 15, 2025 5 min read — views

The term digital twin comes from engineering, where it means a simulation of a physical object that is kept in step with the object's own measurements, so that a change can be tried on the model before it is made to the real thing. In medicine the object is a patient, or one organ or cell type of a patient, and the change is a treatment. The idea is old; the data to build such models for individual patients is recent.

Note: This article describes published research. It is not medical advice. JQ1, the compound in the example, is a research tool and not an approved treatment.

Digital twins in medicine

Several fields have working examples. In cardiology, models of a patient's heart built from imaging and electrical recordings are used to plan ablation and device therapy [1]. In type 1 diabetes, a simulator of 300 virtual patients spanning the measured variability of glucose metabolism was accepted by the FDA in 2008 as a substitute for animal trials when testing closed-loop insulin controllers [2]. In immunology, models of the immune response to viral infection are being built to test interventions per patient [3]. In systems medicine, network models of individual patients are treated computationally with thousands of drugs to rank candidates [4]. A 2024 scoping review maps the current landscape across specialties [5].

What the examples share is a set of four parts: a mechanistic model of the biology; measurements from the individual patient that set the model's parameters; an intervention that can be simulated; and a measured outcome to compare the simulation against. Without the fourth part a twin is a hypothesis, not a tool.

A twin of a leukemia cell

The Beat AML study of a BET inhibitor is a complete example with all four parts [6]. The model is a network of the signaling, metabolic, epigenetic, and transcriptional pathways of a cancer cell, written as ordinary differential equations and assembled from published experimental data: about 112 pathways, over 75,000 reactions, and 3,300 cancer-relevant genes. The patient measurements are conventional cytogenetics and whole-exome sequencing from 100 patients in the Beat AML cohort. Each finding is entered into the network as its functional consequence. A variant is called deleterious when more than five prediction algorithms from dbNSFP agree; a deleterious variant in an oncogene is entered as a gain of function and one in a tumor suppressor as a loss. Genes in a deleted chromosome segment are knocked down, genes in an amplified segment are over-expressed. Solving the network with these changes in place gives the patient's steady state: a map of which pathways are more or less active than in a non-malignant baseline.

The intervention is JQ1, entered as inhibition of its targets BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4, with the downstream consequences taken from the literature, chiefly reduced transcription of MYC and other BRD4-dependent genes. The drug is applied across a range of doses and its effect is summarized as a disease inhibition score (DIS): the percentage reduction in a proliferation index (the active CDK4-cyclin D1, CDK2-cyclin E, CDK2-cyclin A, and CDK1-cyclin B1 complexes) combined with the reduction in a viability index (survival markers AKT1, BCL2, MCL1, BIRC5, BIRC2, and XIAP relative to caspases, PUMA, and cleaved PARP). The measured outcome is the ex vivo sensitivity of the same patient's cells to JQ1, reported as an IC50 from a three-day assay.

Reading the result as four quadrants

Each patient is one point on a plot of predicted DIS against measured IC50. Two thresholds divide the plot. On the measured axis, an IC50 below 2.7 micromolar counts as sensitive; the value is the peak plasma concentration of JQ1 from pharmacokinetic studies, so it separates concentrations a patient could reach from those a patient could not. On the predicted axis, a DIS of 30 percent or more counts as a predicted responder. The thresholds give four quadrants.

Of the 100 patients, 89 were sensitive in the assay and 11 resistant. The model placed 84 of the 89 in the sensitive quadrant and 9 of the 11 in the resistant one, for 93 matches. The seven mismatches split unevenly: five sensitive samples were predicted resistant, and two resistant samples were predicted sensitive. From the table, sensitivity was 94.4 percent and specificity 81.8 percent; the positive predictive value was 97.7 percent and the negative predictive value 64.3 percent [6]. Two cautions apply when reading these. The cohort was heavily weighted toward sensitivity, so the small resistant group carries most of the information about whether the model can tell the two apart. And the negative predictive value is the weakest figure: when the model called a sample resistant it was right about two times in three.

What the twin showed about the drug

The number that validates a twin is the agreement rate. The result that makes it useful is the mechanism, because the network can be read back to see why each patient landed where it did. JQ1 acts on the BET proteins that hold chromatin open at oncogene promoters; blocking BRD4 lowers MYC transcription. The responder profiles in the paper show the routes into that mechanism. One profile carried gains in BRD2 and BRD4 themselves, a loss of DUSP6 that leaves ERK active and MYC high, and a loss of MTHFR that affects chromatin methylation. Another carried a loss of NPM1, which normally restrains BRD4; losing it releases the BRD4-dependent transcriptional program that JQ1 then shuts off. The resistant profiles show the routes around it. One had lost EP300, the acetylase that recruits BRD4 to chromatin, so there was less BRD4 activity for JQ1 to remove, together with an amplified AMPK subunit that promotes a survival response to the drug. Another carried a gain in FGFR4, a receptor kinase that keeps a parallel growth pathway running regardless of BET inhibition [6].

Grouped across the cohort, the same picture held. Mutations that deregulate the ERK pathway (NRAS in 23 patients, DUSP6 in 9, KRAS in 7, NF1 in 6) sat predominantly in the responder group, and every patient with del(7q) or monosomy 7, trisomy 8, or del(5q) responded in both the model and the assay. Trisomy 8 fits the mechanism directly, since MYC is on chromosome 8 [6]. These are hypotheses for a trial's inclusion criteria, arrived at by reading the model rather than by fitting the outcome.

Where the twin stops

A twin of this kind contains only the biology curated into it; a variant with no known functional effect contributes nothing, and the paper attributes its false predictions to that and to incomplete genomic data. The input was DNA alone, so two patients with the same mutation list receive the same twin. The measured outcome was a laboratory assay, not clinical response; the same platform was later tested prospectively in 50 patients with AML or myelodysplastic syndrome, predicting the response to the prescribed treatment correctly in 55 of 61 cases [7]. Within those limits the leukemia example does what the engineering definition asks: a model set from one patient's measurements, an intervention tried in the model, and a measured outcome that the model was checked against.
