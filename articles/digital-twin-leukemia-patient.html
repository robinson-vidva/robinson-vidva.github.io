What a digital twin of a leukemia patient actually is

Building a patient-specific pathway model from a tumor genome and testing it against measured drug response

Research Precision Medicine April 15, 2025 5 min read — views

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is genetically heterogeneous. Two patients with the same diagnosis can carry different combinations of mutations, and a drug that works for one may fail for the other. Sequencing a patient's leukemia is now routine, but a list of mutations does not by itself say which drug to use. One way to bridge that gap is to build a mechanistic model of the leukemia cell from the mutation list and to simulate treatment in the model. In the Beat AML modeling studies this patient-specific model was called a digital twin. The term needs a precise definition, because it is used elsewhere for anything from a spreadsheet of patient records to a whole-body physiological simulation.

Note: This article describes published research methods. It is not medical advice. JQ1, the compound discussed, is a research tool and not an approved treatment.

Building the model

The starting point is a generic model of a myeloid cell: a network of several thousand proteins, metabolites, and genes connected by the reactions and regulatory relationships described in the literature. Receptor and kinase signaling, transcriptional control, cell-cycle progression, apoptosis, DNA damage response, and central metabolism are all represented. Each relationship is written as an equation, so that the activity of one node depends on the activities of the nodes that regulate it. Solving the whole system gives a steady state, a level for every node in an unmutated, untreated cell.

A patient's twin is made by editing this generic model with the patient's genomic findings. Each somatic mutation or copy-number change is entered as its known functional consequence rather than as a label. A FLT3 internal tandem duplication is represented as a constitutively active receptor tyrosine kinase. A truncating mutation in TP53 is represented as loss of p53 transcriptional activity. A deletion of 7q lowers the expression of the genes in that region. When the model is solved again with these changes in place, it reaches a new steady state. The difference between the two states is the patient-specific protein network map: which pathways are more active and which are less active than in a normal myeloid cell, as a consequence of that patient's mutations [1].

Simulating treatment

A drug enters the model through its targets. JQ1, the BET inhibitor used in the study, blocks the bromodomain proteins BRD2, BRD3, and BRD4, and through BRD4 reduces transcription of MYC and other BRD4-dependent genes. Simulating JQ1 therefore means lowering BET activity in the patient's model by an amount that corresponds to the dose, and solving again. The effect is read from a set of nodes that define the leukemia phenotype, chiefly markers of proliferation and cell survival. The changes in those markers are combined into a single disease inhibition score, and a score above a preset threshold classifies the patient as a predicted responder [1].

Because a simulation costs only computing time, the same twin can be run against many drugs and combinations, which is the main practical reason for building one. A laboratory sensitivity assay needs viable cells from the patient and can test only the drugs on the plate.

Validation against measured drug sensitivity

The Beat AML program made the approach testable. It collected 672 samples from 562 AML patients, sequenced them (whole exome and RNA), and measured ex vivo sensitivity to a panel of drugs on the same samples [2]. Genome and drug response are therefore available for each patient.

One hundred patients were selected at random from the cohort. A twin was built for each from the sequencing data, JQ1 was simulated, and the prediction was recorded without reference to the sensitivity data. Comparison with the measured ex vivo IC50 gave agreement in 93 of 100 patients. Sensitivity was 97.7 percent and specificity 64.3 percent [1].

The two figures should be read separately. A sensitivity of 97.7 percent means that almost every sample that responded in the assay was predicted to respond. A specificity of 64.3 percent means that about a third of the samples that did not respond were also predicted to respond. The model overcalls response. For prioritizing patients for a trial that bias is acceptable; for selecting a single drug for a single patient it is not.

Grouping the results by genotype gave a second output. Samples with del(7q) or monosomy 7, trisomy 8, del(5q), or mutations that activate the ERK pathway responded to JQ1 in both the model and the assay [1]. That is a specific, testable hypothesis about which patients a BET-inhibitor trial should enroll, and it came from the model's mechanism rather than from a statistical association alone.

A prospective test

Ex vivo sensitivity is a laboratory surrogate for clinical response. The same platform was also tested prospectively, in a pilot study of patients with myelodysplastic syndrome or AML (NCT02435550). Predictions were generated from genomic data for 50 evaluable patients while blinded to the treatment each patient received. Of 61 patient-treatment pairs, 55 were predicted correctly, an accuracy of 90 percent, with sensitivity of 94 percent and specificity of 88 percent. Laboratory measurement of the predicted pathway activation agreed in 17 of 19 samples [3].

In further Beat AML analyses the same modeling approach was applied to venetoclax, CDK4/6 inhibitors, dasatinib, and BET inhibitors combined with palbociclib or sorafenib [4], [5]. In a separate line of work, models of single cell types were combined to predict the cytokine profiles of inflamed tissue and of a multiple myeloma microenvironment, and checked against co-culture measurements [6].

Limitations

  • The model contains only the biology that has been curated into it. A variant of unknown functional effect cannot be represented and is ignored.
  • The input was DNA only: mutations and copy number. Expression data were not used, so two patients with identical mutation lists receive identical twins.
  • A specificity of 64 percent in the ex vivo comparison means that a predicted response is a reason to test a drug, not a reason to prescribe it.
  • The prospective study enrolled 50 patients. Larger, controlled studies are needed before such predictions can guide treatment.

Within those limits the definition is clear. A digital twin in this sense is a patient-specific steady-state model of leukemia-cell signaling, built from the tumor genome, that can be perturbed with a drug. Against measured drug sensitivity it agreed 93 percent of the time, and against clinical response in a small prospective study 90 percent. It is a tool for ranking candidate drugs and for identifying genomic groups likely to respond, and that is how it should be used.
